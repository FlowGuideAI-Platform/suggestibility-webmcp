# Migration Plan: Single-Region to Multi-Region Active-Active Postgres

**Document owner:** Staff Engineer, Data Platform
**Status:** Approved for Phase 1 (dual-write infrastructure); Phase 3 cutover pending final sign-off
**Last updated:** 2026-07-22
**Audience:** Data Platform, Payments, SRE, Security, Engineering Leadership
**Related tickets:** DATA-4471 (residency commitments), DATA-4502 (replica lag SLOs), SEC-1188 (KMS region topology)

## 1. Summary

Ledgerloop's accounts-payable platform currently runs on a single Aurora PostgreSQL 15 cluster in `us-east-1`: one writer, two cross-AZ reader replicas, roughly 2.4 TB of live data across ~40M invoices and 900M line-item rows. This has been sufficient for three years, but two forcing functions now make single-region untenable:

1. **Data residency.** EU customers are now 18% of ARR and growing faster than the North American book. Several of our largest EU accounts have added data-residency riders to their renewal contracts, and our legal team has flagged that our current cross-border transfer posture (storing EU invoice and remittance data in `us-east-1`) is a renewal risk for at least four accounts worth $2.1M ARR combined, citing GDPR Article 44 transfer restrictions.
2. **Latency and availability.** p95 API latency for EU-based approvers is 340ms above our NA baseline, driven by the transatlantic round trip on every approval-workflow read. Our current DR posture (cross-AZ only) also gives us an RTO of "however long it takes AWS to fail us-east-1 back," which the board has correctly identified as an unacceptable answer during our Series C diligence.

This document describes the plan to move from single-region to an **active-active topology across `us-east-1` and `eu-west-1`**, using tenant-pinned home regions, logical replication for cross-region read visibility, and a phased dual-write cutover. It covers the target architecture, the migration phases, the cutover runbook, the rollback plan, and the consistency trade-offs we are accepting to hit our Q3 commitment to the EU accounts referenced above.

This is being treated internally as a one-way door: once tenant data has been rehomed and both regions are accepting writes for their respective tenant sets, reverting to single-region requires either a second migration project or accepting data loss. Section 8 covers what we are and are not guaranteeing, and Section 10 is the risk register we are asking Engineering Leadership and Security to sign off on explicitly, not just Data Platform.

## 2. Current Architecture

```
                      us-east-1
                 ┌─────────────────────┐
  API tier  ───► │  Aurora PG 15        │
  (ECS, 3 AZ)     │  1 writer, 2 readers │
                 │  db.r6g.4xlarge      │
                 └─────────────────────┘
                          │
                          ▼
                 nightly WAL archive → S3
                 (cross-region copy to eu-west-1,
                  cold storage only, not queryable)
```

- Primary keys are `bigserial` on the majority of tables (invoices, line_items, approvals, remittances). A handful of newer tables (webhooks, audit_log) already use ULIDs.
- Invoice numbers are generated by a Postgres sequence per legal entity (`invoice_seq_<entity_id>`), incremented at insert time, and are relied upon by the billing/tax module to be **gapless and monotonically increasing** per entity — several of our EU entities are subject to e-invoicing rules that expect exactly this property.
- The reporting pipeline (CDC via Debezium → Kafka → Snowflake) reads the single writer's WAL. It is not region-aware today because there is only one region.
- Background jobs (Sidekiq-equivalent, our internal `jobrunner` service) talk to the writer directly for job state; there is no job queue replication story today.

## 3. Target Architecture

```
        us-east-1                              eu-west-1
   ┌─────────────────────┐               ┌─────────────────────┐
   │  Aurora PG 15         │◄──logical───►│  Aurora PG 15         │
   │  writer (NA tenants)  │  replication  │  writer (EU tenants)  │
   │  2 readers            │  (both ways,  │  2 readers            │
   └─────────────────────┘  filtered by   └─────────────────────┘
              │              tenant_id)              │
              └──────────────┬───────────────────────┘
                              ▼
                   Region-aware routing layer
                   (tenant → home_region lookup,
                    cached in Redis per-region,
                    source of truth in a small
                    global config table replicated
                    to both regions)
```

Key architectural decisions:

- **Tenant affinity, not sharding by row.** Every tenant (a customer company) is assigned a single home region at signup or during migration. All writes for that tenant's data go to its home region's writer. This is the single biggest simplifying decision in this plan — it avoids building general-purpose multi-region conflict resolution, because in the common case, two regions are never writing to the same tenant's rows concurrently.
- **Logical replication, not physical.** We're using Postgres native logical replication (not Aurora Global Database) because Aurora Global Database is single-writer with a read-only secondary region, which doesn't give us active-active. Native logical replication lets each region publish its own tenant-partitioned tables and subscribe to the other region's, filtered by a `home_region` column so each region only pulls in rows it doesn't own, for read purposes.
- **ID generation moves off `bigserial`.** Any table that can be written from either region (see Section 4.2) moves to 64-bit Snowflake-style IDs: a timestamp, a region tag, and a per-node counter, generated in application code before insert, not by the database. Tables that are genuinely single-writer per row for their whole lifetime (most of them, given tenant affinity) can stay on `bigserial` scoped per region without collision, because a NA-homed tenant's rows only ever get inserted by the NA writer.
- **Global objects are the exception, not the rule.** A small number of entities aren't cleanly tenant-scoped: the cross-tenant admin search index, the platform-wide webhook delivery log, and a "workspace merge" feature (used when two customers combine after an acquisition and want their Ledgerloop tenants merged) that can, briefly, involve simultaneous writes from both regions to overlapping data. We are treating these as known exceptions with a simpler, weaker guarantee — see Section 4.3.

## 4. Design Decisions and Trade-offs

### 4.1 Tenant-to-region assignment

New tenants are assigned a home region by their contracting entity's registered country at signup. Existing tenants are assigned based on the mailing address on file, with EU-flagged tenants (per the residency-rider list from Legal) migrated first.

Tenants can request a region move (e.g., a US company acquiring an EU subsidiary that wants everything in one place). A region move is a full data migration for that tenant — copy all rows, cut over their `home_region` pointer, tombstone the old copy after a verification window. We are scoping this as a manual, ops-assisted process for the first year rather than self-service, given the blast radius of getting it wrong.

### 4.2 ID generation

Tables touched by cross-tenant features (see 4.3) move to the Snowflake-style ID scheme described above. This is roughly 12 tables out of ~140. The remaining tables keep `bigserial`, which is simpler to reason about and keeps existing foreign keys, ORM code, and pagination logic working unchanged. We spent real time debating whether to just move everything to the new ID scheme for consistency and to avoid a two-tier mental model for engineers — ultimately decided against it because touching all 140 tables meant touching every write path in the monolith, which was a multi-quarter project on its own and not something we could justify against the Q3 residency deadline. This means the codebase will carry two ID conventions indefinitely, and any table that starts single-region-only but later grows a cross-region feature will need an ID migration of its own. We think this is an acceptable and fairly common trade-off, but it is a trade-off, not a free lunch.

### 4.3 Conflict resolution for cross-tenant objects

For the ~12 tables that can receive writes from both regions concurrently, we're using **last-write-wins by wall-clock timestamp**, with the timestamp taken from the application server, not the database, to keep it consistent across regions to within our NTP-synced clock skew budget (we're targeting <50ms skew, monitored via a synthetic canary that writes a timestamped row to both regions every 10 seconds and alerts if the observed skew exceeds 200ms).

This is the decision we expect the most pushback on, and we want to be upfront about why we made it rather than building proper conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs) or a consensus layer for these tables. The honest answer is that the affected tables are low-write-volume (the workspace-merge feature runs maybe twice a month platform-wide) and the cost of a wrong resolution — an admin search index entry briefly pointing at a stale record, a webhook delivery logged out of order — is genuinely low for every table except one: the workspace-merge audit trail, where LWW could silently drop a legitimate concurrent edit made by two admins in different regions during a merge window. We've mitigated this by adding a UI-level lock (only one admin can be "in" a merge operation at a time, enforced by a short-lived advisory lock replicated via the global config table) rather than solving it at the data layer. Whether that's sufficient given the audit and compliance weight of merge operations is something we want Security's explicit sign-off on, because we're aware "we added a UI lock" is a weaker guarantee than "the data model can't diverge."

### 4.4 Invoice numbering across regions

This is the sharpest edge in the whole plan. Several EU legal entities require gapless, strictly monotonic invoice numbers — a regulator can and does ask "show me you have no gaps." Our current single-sequence-per-entity design gives us that for free because there's one writer.

Under active-active, if a legal entity's invoices could theoretically be written from either region, we'd need a distributed counter with strict ordering, which defeats a lot of the point of active-active (it becomes a bottleneck and a single point of coordination again). We're avoiding that problem by construction: **a legal entity's invoice sequence lives entirely in one region**, tied to the entity's `home_region`, same as tenant affinity above. This works today because we don't have any legal entity that needs to issue invoices from both regions simultaneously.

The unaddressed risk here — and we're flagging it explicitly because we think it's the least defensible part of this plan under scrutiny — is that this assumption is a product assumption, not a technical guarantee, and nothing in the system enforces it. If a customer's EU subsidiary starts issuing invoices under the same legal entity ID as their US parent (which Sales has told us "basically never happens" but has not ruled out contractually), we would silently get either sequence gaps or a race condition on the counter, and we would likely not find out until an auditor or a customer's finance team did. There is no monitoring in this plan that would catch a legal entity accumulating writes from both regions before it becomes a compliance incident. We think this is an acceptable risk to accept for launch given the "basically never happens" input from Sales, but we want to say clearly that "basically never" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and we have not built the guardrail that would make it "never."

### 4.5 Key management topology

Encryption keys (Aurora's KMS-backed storage encryption, plus application-level envelope encryption for bank account numbers and tax IDs) currently live in a single `us-east-1` KMS key. For the EU cluster, we're provisioning a **separate regional KMS key in `eu-west-1`**, which most of Security's residency guidance treats as required (key material shouldn't leave the region whose data it protects). The open question, which we're bringing to Security rather than deciding unilaterally, is what happens for **break-glass DR**: if `eu-west-1` becomes fully unavailable and we need to restore EU tenant data into `us-east-1` temporarily, do we pre-provision a way for `us-east-1` to unwrap EU-encrypted fields, or do we accept that a `eu-west-1` regional outage means EU tenants are unavailable until the region recovers, full stop, with no cross-region failover for their data at all? Pre-provisioning that access is operationally simpler and gives us a real DR story, but it means a `us-east-1` KMS compromise now has a plausible path to EU key material, which is close to the exact thing the EU key was supposed to prevent. We are inclined toward "no cross-region unwrap, EU tenants ride out an eu-west-1 outage" for the initial launch, accepting a worse DR posture for EU tenants in exchange for a cleaner security boundary, but this hasn't been formally signed off and we know Payments will push back given SLA exposure.
